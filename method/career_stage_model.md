# Resume Signal by Career Stage: A Four-Stage Model

**Status:** Reference. Review loop item 1 uses it: name the stage a draft reads at, and compare it with the stage the target role sits at. A Senior Director or VP target should read at Stage 3 in the current role.

**Evidence note.** The stage tables are synthesis, not study results. They draw on practitioner consensus about senior resumes and on general knowledge of how executive search works. No published study grades resumes as table stakes, good or exceptional. Read it as a model to test, not a finding. It never overrides `profile/locked_truth.md` or Rule A1: wanting a bullet to read as exceptional is never a reason to stretch a claim.

**How to use it.** Find the target role's stage. For each dimension (scope, command, wins, impact), check whether the current-role block reaches at least "good" at that stage. Where it reads a stage lower, the fix is usually removing operator detail, not adding words (see `rules.md`, section B).

---

## 1. Why there is so little guidance above Director

These four reasons are inference. They fit the pattern in the research but are not proven.

1. **The audience is too small to pay for content.** Resume advice is funded by volume: job boards, resume services and career coaches. Every VP seat has dozens of entry-level seats below it. The free advice follows the volume.
2. **The resume stops being the screen.** At VP and above, most hires come through networks, referrals and retained search. The resume is checked after someone has already vouched for you, so it confirms a judgment rather than creating one. Little gets written about a document that rarely decides the outcome.
3. **Search firms rewrite you anyway.** A retained search firm presents each finalist to the client in its own write-up: career summary, assessment against the role, references. Its value is that judgment, so it has no reason to publish a playbook. This matches the research: Korn Ferry, Spencer Stuart, Heidrick and Russell Reynolds publish nothing current on resumes.
4. **Fit is specific to the mandate.** A VP hire is judged against one problem: build, scale, turnaround or integration. A resume that wins a turnaround role can lose a build role. That resists a universal template.

**What it means:** at Sr. Director and VP, the resume's main job is to pass the recruiter's or search partner's first read, then hold up in the interview. Network and referral paths matter more than any formatting choice at this level.

## 2. EVP and C-suite: what changes

- **The channel is retained search and board networks.** Some hires come from the CEO's or board's own network. Private-equity-backed companies use their operating partners' networks. Cold applications are rare. Inference: firms are paid a retainer, conventionally about a third of first-year cash compensation, so they control who gets presented.
- **The document set changes.** A one-page executive bio (narrative, third person) often travels further than the resume. Board decks, references and the search firm's write-up carry the weight. The resume runs two to three pages and is a reference document.
- **Each role is introduced by its company.** One line per employer gives revenue, headcount, ownership (public, PE-backed or private) and the situation you inherited. The reader needs the scale of the company before the scale of the job.
- **The measures become enterprise value.** Revenue and EBITDA growth, margin, shareholder return, market share, deals closed and integrated, capital raised. Functional metrics drop out unless they tie to these.
- **New content appears:** board and investor interaction, transformation, culture change, and succession built (who you developed into senior roles).

**For a functional career:** the top of most functions is VP or SVP. A chief officer seat for the function exists only at some large companies. The C-suite step usually runs through a general management, strategy or adjacent chief role. Treat C-suite as a different career move, not the next rung.

## 3. The model

**One rule runs across all four stages:**
- **Table stakes** state the fact: what you did, or what you ran.
- **Good** quantifies it with context, so the reader can judge its size.
- **Exceptional** shows cause and consequence: the decision was yours, the business changed because of it, and the result lasted or repeated. It also includes one detail no peer could claim.

What changes across stages is the unit being measured:

| Stage | Unit of work | Core question the reader asks |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Task | Can you do the work well? |
| Mid / Manager to Director | Program or function | Can you run something and make it better? |
| Sr. Director / VP | Organization and agenda | Can you set direction and move the business? |
| EVP / C-suite | Enterprise | Can you create value and lead leaders? |

The four dimensions map to each stage as follows:
- **Scope:** what you are responsible for.
- **Area of command:** who and what you direct.
- **Wins:** discrete achievements.
- **Impact:** the business effect of those wins.

---

### Stage 1: Entry (0 to 3 years)

| | Table stakes | Good | Exceptional |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Names the projects or tasks you owned | Gives volume or complexity: number of projects, markets, clients | Shows you were handed more scope than the role normally gets |
| Command | None expected | Coordinated others or a vendor | Trained others or wrote a process the team adopted |
| Wins | Accomplishments rather than duties | Each win has a number (time, accuracy, volume) | A win a manager would name in a reference |
| Impact | Shows the work was finished | Ties the work to a team outcome | Ties the work to a client or business decision |

### Stage 2: Mid / Manager to Director

| | Table stakes | Good | Exceptional |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Programs, budget and clients named | Dollar size and portfolio share stated | Scope growth over time, showing you were trusted with more |
| Command | Team size | Team structure (layers, functions, offshore) | Built the team or a new function; promoted people out of it |
| Wins | Accomplishment-led, Context-Action-Result (CAR) form | Every win measured | Wins that are firsts: built from scratch, made a standard, productized |
| Impact | Operational metrics: time, cost, quality | Commercial metrics: margin, revenue, retention | A decision the client or business made because of your work |

### Stage 3: Sr. Director / VP

| | Table stakes | Good | Exceptional |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | One-line scope per role: budget, headcount, reporting line | Adds the business it serves and its scale (company revenue, markets) | States the mandate you were hired or promoted to deliver, so the reader sees the brief and the result |
| Command | Multi-layer organization; managers of managers | Org design choices: what you built, merged or cut | Leaders you developed into peer or senior roles elsewhere |
| Wins | The biggest win leads each role | Wins framed as strategic choices and tradeoffs | The same kind of win repeated across roles or companies, so it reads as a pattern you can bring |
| Impact | Business metrics, not functional ones | Executive or enterprise adoption: became the standard, tied to compensation, changed budget allocation | Changed what the company does: strategy, portfolio, investment, a market entered or exited |

### Stage 4: EVP / C-suite

| | Table stakes | Good | Exceptional |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | P&L size, enterprise headcount, geographies | Company context per role: revenue, ownership, situation inherited | Situation you inherited, then the end state, measured in enterprise value |
| Command | Leads senior leaders across functions | Board and investor exposure | Built the executive team; succession that held after you left |
| Wins | Transformation or growth outcomes | Deals: M&A, integration, capital raised | Value-creation events: exit, IPO, turnaround |
| Impact | Revenue, EBITDA, margin | Shareholder return, market share | Outcomes the market or board attributes to you by name |
